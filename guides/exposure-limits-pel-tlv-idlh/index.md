# Exposure Limits Explained: PEL, REL, TLV, STEL, Ceiling and IDLH

HAZWOPER guide · 2 min read · Updated 2026-10-30

HAZWOPER training levels, PPE selection, medical surveillance and air monitoring all depend on **exposure limits**: airborne concentrations a worker can be exposed to without expected harm. Here are the terms you'll see on safety data sheets and in site plans.

## Who sets the limits

| Limit | Set by | Legal status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PEL (Permissible Exposure Limit) | OSHA | Legally enforceable (29 CFR 1910.1000 tables and substance-specific standards) |
| REL (Recommended Exposure Limit) | NIOSH | Recommendation, often stricter than the PEL |
| TLV (Threshold Limit Value) | ACGIH | Guideline, updated yearly; adopted by some states and contracts |
| IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health) | NIOSH | Used for respirator selection and emergency decisions |

Many PELs date from 1971 and are less protective than current RELs and TLVs. HAZWOPER uses PELs and, where there is no PEL, "published exposure levels".

## Types of limits

- **TWA (time-weighted average):** the average concentration over an 8-hour shift (OSHA) or a 10-hour shift (NIOSH)
- **STEL (short-term exposure limit):** a 15-minute average that shouldn't be exceeded at any time in the shift
- **Ceiling (C):** a concentration that must never be exceeded, even briefly
- **Skin notation:** the chemical can be absorbed through skin, so airborne limits alone don't protect workers
- **IDLH:** a concentration that could cause death, immediate or delayed permanent health effects, or prevent escape. Only atmosphere-supplying respirators (SCBA or supplied air with escape) are used at or above IDLH

## Routes of exposure

1. **Inhalation:** the most common route at hazardous waste sites
2. **Skin and eye absorption:** many solvents and pesticides pass through skin
3. **Ingestion:** usually from contaminated hands, food or tobacco, which is why eating and smoking are banned in the exclusion zone
4. **Injection:** punctures from contaminated sharp objects

## Acute vs chronic effects

**Acute** effects show up during or shortly after a short, high exposure, such as dizziness from solvent vapors or burns from a corrosive. **Chronic** effects come from repeated exposure over months or years, such as cancer, liver or kidney damage, or nerve damage. Some substances have both.

## Using limits on site

[Air monitoring](https://www.hazwoperosha.com/guides/air-monitoring/) readings are compared against these limits to choose [PPE levels](https://www.hazwoperosha.com/guides/ppe-levels-a-b-c-d/), set action levels in the [site plan](https://www.hazwoperosha.com/guides/site-safety-and-health-plan/) and decide who needs [medical surveillance](https://www.hazwoperosha.com/guides/hazwoper-medical-surveillance/). A respirator's assigned protection factor times the exposure limit gives the maximum concentration it can be used in.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a PEL and a TLV?

A PEL is OSHA's legally enforceable limit. A TLV is a guideline published by ACGIH that is updated regularly and is often more protective.

### What does IDLH mean?

Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health: a concentration that could cause death or serious permanent harm or prevent escape. Only SCBA or supplied air with escape is used at or above IDLH.

### What is a STEL?

A short-term exposure limit: a 15-minute time-weighted average exposure that should not be exceeded at any time during the workday.
